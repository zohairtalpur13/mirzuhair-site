A bilingual type system · Sindhi and English

Gaḍ گڏ

Gaḍ, گڏ: together

Road signs, shop boards, school books and newspapers in Sindh carry Sindhi and English side by side. Usually one of them is squeezed to fit the other: the Sindhi set too small to read, or its dots clipped by tight lines.

Gaḍ is a small type system that lets the two scripts share a page as equals. It pairs Lateef, a typeface made for Sindhi, with Literata, and gives them one baseline grid, one hierarchy and a mirrored layout that meets at a spine.

02

Three problems

Setting the two scripts with the same settings breaks in three predictable ways. Switch each one between the default and the tuned setting.

Size

Cotton, thread and cloth

ڪپهه، ڌاڳو ۽ ڪپڙو

Sindhi: cotton, thread and cloth

Default: at the same point size, Sindhi reads a size smaller. Tuned: the Sindhi is scaled until the height of its bowls matches the English x-height, 1.66×.

Dots

ڀٽ شاهه ۽ ٿر

ڪپهه، ڌاڳو ۽ ڪپڙو

ڇاڇرو، ڳوٺ ۽ ڦل

Bhit Shah and Thar · cotton, thread and cloth · Chachro, village and fruit

Default: at English line spacing the dots of one line touch the next. Several Sindhi letters carry four dots, like ٿ ڀ ڦ. Tuned: Sindhi gets its own leading, locked to the shared grid.

Direction

Lateef فونٽ سنڌي لاءِ ٺهيل آهي، سنڌي ۾ 52 اکر آهن

The Lateef font is made for Sindhi; Sindhi has 52 letters.

Default: in a left-to-right box the sentence starts at the wrong edge and its comma and number land out of place. Tuned: the paragraph runs right to left, the English name is isolated, and numbers use Sindhi digits.

    The system

    Four rules and one table. Everything sits on a 6 px baseline unit, so the two scripts can run in separate columns and still meet.

    1. One unitEvery line height and every gap is a multiple of 6 px.
    2. Matched bodySindhi is set 1.66× the English size, measured so the bowls of Sindhi letters like ص ع ه match the English x-height.
    3. Nine to fiveEnglish body runs on 30 px lines, Sindhi on 54 px. Nine lines of English and five of Sindhi meet on the same baseline every 270 px.
    4. A mirrored spineEnglish reads from the left edge, Sindhi from the right. The two columns meet at a shared gutter, never stacked under each other.

    English

    Two scripts, one line

    A system for setting Sindhi and English side by side, so that neither has to shrink to fit the other.

    Sindhi has been written in this form of the Arabic script since 1853, when a committee settled on fifty-two letters. Its extra letters carry sounds that English and Urdu readers rarely meet: four implosives, a row of aspirates and retroflexes, and dots stacked four at a time.

    Those dots are the first rule of this page. Sindhi needs more space between lines than English, so the two columns run on different line heights. Both are built from the same six-pixel unit, so every ninth English line lands on the same baseline as every fifth Sindhi one.

    The second rule is size. At the same point size Sindhi looks a step smaller than English. Here it is scaled until the height of its bowls matches the English x-height, so a reader crossing the spine does not feel the page change.

    The Sindhi column carries words and verse; the English column explains the rules.

    Notes

    Sources, fonts and what the page measures.

    • Alphabet Order and sounds follow the Sindhi Wikipedia table of the Arabic-Sindhi alphabet (سنڌي آئيويٽا, the Sindhi alphabet). The 52 letters count the digraphs جه and گه.
    • 1853 The year a committee formed under the East India Company settled the fifty-two-letter alphabet, as recorded in the same article.
    • Verse Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, from Shah jo Risalo, as quoted with its English translation by Dunya News.
    • Scale The Sindhi size is measured, not guessed: Naskh has no x-height, so the page measures the closed bowls of ص ع ه in Lateef and the x-height of Literata in your browser, and divides one by the other: 1.66×.
    • Fonts Lateef by SIL International, named after Shah Abdul Latif and made for Sindhi; Literata by TypeTogether; JetBrains Mono. All under the SIL Open Font License.
