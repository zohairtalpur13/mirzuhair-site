# MIR — What remains.

## Project overview

MIR is a self-initiated fragrance and communication-design concept exploring how a scent can be expressed visually. The campaign translates existing architectural observations and textile drawings into stationery, packaging, typographic posters, a twelve-second motion study and an interactive postcard maker.

The brand is called MIR. The work is a speculative campaign, not a commissioned or manufactured fragrance. It extends an existing textile practice into communication design through a shared interest in contact, repetition and memory.

**Disciplines:** visual identity, typography, art direction, packaging concepts, motion and interaction.

**Central question:** How can a campaign communicate something its audience cannot smell through a screen?

**Campaign line:** What remains.

## 1. Context and brief

The starting portfolio already contained architectural photographs, detailed doorway repeats, painted-ceiling drawings, lantern illustrations and a personal typographic identity. The new project needed to belong to that body of work while demonstrating an expanded communication-design range.

The initial request was for visually ambitious cards and posters. Later references clarified that the desired outcome was a complete identity presentation: an idea expressed through materials, imagery, typography and applications. The brief expanded to include a perfume bottle and an admissions-oriented case study with evidence of problem solving.

The final brief was to develop an original MIR campaign using the existing artwork, create tangible-looking business-card and packaging applications, and extend the concept into working motion and interactive experiments.

## 2. Intended audience

The proposed campaign addresses people interested in design, fragrance and collectible objects. It assumes an audience that values the story and physical character of an object alongside its function. This is a positioning hypothesis, not a conclusion from market interviews.

For the portfolio, the audience is also a reviewer assessing the relationship between the problem, design decisions and result. The case study therefore needs to show why an element exists, not only how it looks.

## 3. The communication problem

There were three linked problems.

First, fragrance has no visible form. A bottle photograph can show packaging, but cannot by itself communicate an experience of scent.

Second, a luxury-looking mockup is easy to separate from the designer's own practice. Without a recognizable source, the campaign could look like a generic exercise in tasteful materials.

Third, simply resizing one design across media would not demonstrate a range of communication thinking. Print, motion and interaction needed different roles.

The resulting objective was to give an invisible experience a visible behavior, connect that behavior to existing work, and adapt it to each medium.

## 4. Reference analysis

The reference projects were studied for presentation methods rather than copied as identity systems.

- Large typography, strong colour contrast and close attention to card surfaces.
- Controlled light and shadow that make paper feel like an object.
- A coherent identity carried through emblem, colour, embossing and applications, presented as one continuous project.

The relevant lesson was consistency between concept, surface and presentation. MIR's imagery instead comes from my own doorway and ceiling drawings. It does not reuse any reference's emblem, typography or identity.

## 5. Source material and interpretation

The doorway source photograph establishes the architectural context. The existing doorway line repeat turns that context into a graphic surface. The existing ceiling drawing provides a second, radial vocabulary for the interactive piece. The lantern illustration provides a distinct third interaction option.

The important transformation is from an existing textile repeat to a communication system. The drawing does not have to be simplified into a new emblem to be useful: it can occupy a card reverse, a carton panel, an envelope surface or a masked area of an interactive composition.

The original raster drawings are used directly in the downloadable card artwork and digital pieces. The mockups interpret those drawings on physical surfaces; their details are not exact production reproductions.

## 6. Concept: a lasting impression

A scent can suggest a person or place after the encounter has ended. The campaign interprets that idea through a visible trace.

On paper, a trace is pressure, relief or transferred ink. In motion, it is an afterimage. In interaction, it is a mark that persists after the visitor's hand or pointer moves away.

This leads to the line “What remains.” The phrase is short enough to work on a card and open enough to support several visual interpretations. It also connects to the existing portfolio's interest in carrying architectural memory forward through textiles.

The supporting lines are creative campaign copy:

- A sleeve after an embrace.
- A name after departure.
- An impression that remains.

They are not claims about a tested fragrance formulation.

## 7. Actual development and course corrections

### Early approach: template-led presentation

An Adobe Express cover was produced under the working title Inherited Marks. I rejected it as unsuitable. It offered a presentation surface before establishing an object, a distinct concept or a meaningful connection to the existing artwork.

**Decision:** move to a business-card and perfume relationship, using stronger art direction and my actual visual sources.

### First perfume and stationery visualization

A green glass bottle, ivory card, embossed reverse and oxblood edge established a material direction. The initial naming used the designer's full name; I decided that the brand should be MIR only.

**Decision:** shorten the identity to MIR, replace the reverse Z with M and remove the old name-based URL. This was a direct brief correction, not an aesthetic claim that one name is inherently better.

### From mockup to concept

I pushed for more conceptual depth and a broader range of communication design. “What remains.” became the organizing idea. Three typographic routes explored pressure, transfer and absence.

**Decision:** give each medium a distinct way to express a trace, rather than relying on repeated bottle photographs.

### From generic material styling to existing artwork

The latest stationery and packaging visuals use the doorway drawing as a defining graphic element. The digital pieces use the original doorway and ceiling files directly.

**Decision:** keep the wordmark quiet and let the existing artwork provide specificity. This makes the relationship to the portfolio visible rather than merely asserted in the text.

## 8. Visual identity decisions

### Wordmark

The editable MIR signature uses high-contrast strokes and a broad, stable silhouette. The three letters allow a clear identity at larger sizes without competing with intricate illustration. The vector mark is a new campaign drawing, not a verified extraction of the prior Quiet Structure wordmark.

### Color

Paper (#EEE9DF) gives the line drawings a warm ground. Carbon (#20221F) provides clear type and dark surfaces. Vermilion (#D4422E) creates a recognizable campaign accent and distinguishes new communication pieces from the earlier green-led render.

Green glass and oxblood remain object materials in the perfume visualization. The graphic system itself stays disciplined around paper, carbon and vermilion.

### Hierarchy

The brand name is dominant on a small object. Descriptive copy is secondary. Fine ornament is placed where it can be seen at a useful scale. On posters, one typographic event carries the message; repeated decorative devices are avoided.

### Texture

Texture supports the idea of an impression. It belongs to paper, printing and cloth rather than being an all-over effect applied to every surface. Exact finish depth, stock and ink behavior would require physical testing.

## 9. Print and object applications

### Business cards

The downloadable 85 × 55 mm face uses MIR on vermilion with “What remains.” The reverse pairs the original doorway repeat with a readable discipline line and location. No invented email, phone number or website is supplied.

The stationery mockup explores thick paper, an embossed black envelope and a wax seal. These establish material intent; the separate SVG files are the editable artwork masters.

### Scent strip

A 25 × 150 mm strip carries the MIR identity and a vermilion tip. It gives fragrance sampling its own functional format while maintaining a family relationship with the business card.

### Packaging

The bottle is paired with a carton whose illustrated surface carries the doorway into the object system. The front preserves a clear product hierarchy while the side panel uses a stronger repeat.

This is a packaging visualization. A production carton still requires a structural dieline, dimensions, ingredient and regulatory content, barcode where applicable, and manufacturer review.

### Textile label

A 60 × 30 mm label carries the identity into a proposed textile sleeve. The sleeve idea connects to the existing practice and can become a later material prototype. The campaign does not claim that this sleeve has been manufactured.

## 10. Typographic experiments

**Pressure:** a large M and a tonal relief study turn a letter into an object. The visual asks whether an impression can remain recognizable with very little color contrast.

**Transfer:** one offset impression follows the MIR signature. The displacement gives the idea of contact a typographic form. The precise lettering remains legible while the second impression supplies movement.

**Absence:** the identity is reduced to M and R around a narrow vermilion line. The missing I becomes a space to notice. The reading depends on familiarity with MIR, so this works best within the campaign rather than as an isolated first exposure.

The three posters deliberately test different means of communicating the same idea. They are not three unrelated visual styles.

## 11. Motion: a trace through time

The working twelve-second canvas film uses a four-part sequence:

1. 0–3 seconds: the doorway pattern appears with “A room. An impression.”
2. 3–7 seconds: MIR moves across the field with delayed impressions.
3. 7–10 seconds: the impressions fade and “What remains.” takes over.
4. 10–12 seconds: the film resolves to MIR and the campaign line.

Playback is initiated by the visitor, with pause, restart, loop and timeline controls. A frame can be saved as PNG. A browser with MediaRecorder support can export the complete twelve-second video.

The film is silent. Motion is carried by typographic displacement, fading and contrast rather than unsupported claims about sound design. A reviewer can inspect any point using the timeline without playing the animation.

## 12. Interaction: an impression made by the visitor

The postcard maker invites a visitor to leave marks across the canvas. Each gesture reveals a circular fragment of an existing drawing. The visitor can choose Ceiling, Doorway or Lantern, change mark scale and set persistence to six seconds, fifteen seconds or indefinite.

The default is a static, persistent composition. This allows deliberate making and avoids forcing fading motion. Optional persistence introduces time as a design variable.

Mouse, touch and keyboard input are supported. Arrow keys move the working position; Space or Enter stamps a mark. A separate button places a central impression. Reset clears the surface and Save exports a 1600 × 900 PNG without the editing cursor.

The functional relationship to the campaign is direct: something remains after the visitor's gesture. The image becomes a small souvenir, extending the physical scent-card idea into a screen-based experience.

## 13. Marketing structure

**Encounter:** typographic posters establish the phrase and visual tension.

**Explore:** the short film demonstrates the identity's behavior and introduces the idea of a lingering impression.

**Participate:** the interactive postcard allows a visitor to create within the campaign's visual rules.

**Keep:** the business card, scent strip and textile application suggest a physical continuation.

This is a proposed campaign sequence, not a report of a real launch. No reach, conversion, sales or research metrics are invented.

## 14. What to evaluate

Technical checks are recorded separately in VALIDATION.md. They evaluate the built artifact, not audience comprehension.

The next human-feedback study should ask a small group to describe the idea before reading the rationale, identify the brand on each application and complete the postcard interaction using their preferred input method. Record the exact responses, not only favorable quotes.

Physical evaluation should include actual-size card prints, fine-line reproduction, contrast on selected stock and a printer's assessment of embossing. Mark those tests as complete only after they occur.

## 15. Reflection

The main improvement was moving from a material mood to a repeatable idea. The first bottle-and-card visual established atmosphere; the expanded campaign makes a trace behave through print, time and interaction.

The strongest connection to the existing practice is the reuse of real drawings, especially where they remain intact in the flat artwork and digital tools. The most important limitation is the gap between material mockups and physical production. The next round should resolve that gap through print tests and consistent typesetting, then gather evidence about how the audience understands the campaign.
